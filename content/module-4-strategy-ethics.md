# Strategy, money and ethics

*Module 4 · about 40 minutes · artefact: `strategy/business-case.md`*

## What you are making

A one-page case for one AI change in your work: what it costs, what it saves, who could be harmed, and the
rule that stops the harm.

## Why it matters

Half the AI projects in Australian businesses this year will be approved because someone was enthusiastic
and cancelled because nobody measured anything. The page you write today is the difference between being
the person who bought a tool and the person who made a case.

## Where the money actually is

Four honest sources of value, in descending order of how often they are real:

| Source | Looks like | Usually worth |
|---|---|---|
| **Time returned** | Hours a week back from repetitive drafting, sorting, checking | Real, if you name the hours and the person |
| **Quality floor** | Fewer missed details, fewer returns, fewer angry emails | Real, if you can count the returns |
| **Speed to first draft** | A proposal in twenty minutes instead of two hours | Real for bids; often imaginary for internal work |
| **New capability** | Something you could not do at all before | Rare, and usually the only one worth a project |

The fake ones to name and refuse: "productivity" with no hours attached, "innovation" with no artefact, and
any saving that assumes a person's entire salary disappears.

:::callout note The arithmetic nobody does
A tool that saves four hours a week for one person is roughly $12,000 a year at a loaded rate of $60 an hour.
A tool that costs $30 a seat for twelve people is $4,320 a year, before the hours spent learning it, fixing
its mistakes and supervising it. Most "obvious" wins are one careful page away from being obvious losses.
:::

## Risk, in the language of the people who sign things off

Build the register before someone builds it for you:

| Class | Example | Control that satisfies an auditor |
|---|---|---|
| **Customer data** | Pasting a client's details into a chat window | Written rule, redaction habit, approved tools only |
| **Personal information** | Staff records, health details | Not in prompts at all, or a local model |
| **Commercial** | Pricing, contracts, margins | Approved tools only, and no training on your data |
| **Public claims** | Anything a customer will read | Human review before publication, always |
| **Decisions about people** | Hiring, credit, performance | No automated decision without appeal and a named human |

Two Australian specifics worth knowing without pretending to be a lawyer: the Privacy Act applies to
personal information wherever it is processed, and AI-assisted decisions about individuals are already
attracting regulatory attention. Your control is the same either way: know where the data went, and be able
to show a human decided.

## Ethics that are not decoration

- **Disclosure**: say when something was drafted with AI, especially to clients and in anything published.
- **Reversibility**: prefer the version of the workflow you can undo.
- **Attention**: if a person's job becomes supervising a machine 40 hours a week, that is not a saving, it is
  a worse job with a spreadsheet attached.
- **The quiet harms**: a model that erodes a junior's chance to learn by doing the learning for them.

## The check

The agent reads the case and confirms:

1. One change, this quarter, with a named person whose hours change.
2. Value expressed in hours and dollars, assumptions shown, no "productivity" without a number.
3. Costs include learning and supervision time.
4. Five risk rows, each mapped to a class and a control that is operational, not aspirational.
5. A disclosure rule that is specific enough to be broken.
6. The thirty-word sentence is under thirty words and contains no jargon.

Failure signs: a case that would be approved by anyone, a register with no customer data class, or controls
of the form "be careful".

## Sealed when

your progress file.
