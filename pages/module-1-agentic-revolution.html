The agentic revolution

What agents actually are, and the loop they run

Module 1 of 5About 40 minArtefact position/agent-map.md

A one-page map of where agents already touch your work, what they still cannot do, and the first task you will hand to one.

Why it matters

Two people will lose their jobs in the next two years: the one who thought agents were magic, and the one who thought they were nothing. The map you make today is how you avoid both, because it separates what an agent can genuinely carry from what only looks like it can.

What an agent actually is

An agent is a model with four things bolted on. Remove any one and you are back to a chatbot:

PartWhat it givesWhat happens without it
ModelJudgement and languageNothing runs at all
ToolsHands: files, terminal, browser, emailIt can only talk about the work
MemoryContinuity between sessionsEvery conversation starts cold
A loopThe ability to act, look, and act againOne-shot answers, no follow-through

The loop is what people underestimate. A chatbot answers; an agent tries, checks the result against reality, and adjusts. When you see an agent do something impressive, it is almost always the loop, not the model.

The difference in one sentence

A chatbot tells you how to check a spreadsheet. An agent opens the spreadsheet, checks it, tells you what it found, and asks before it changes anything.

Where agents are genuinely good

  • Reading a lot and summarising honestly, with the source attached.
  • Turning a rough instruction into a finished artefact: a page, a letter, a script, a table.
  • Doing the same judgement many times in a row, consistently, without getting bored.
  • Checking their own work when you tell them how: running the test, opening the page, comparing the totals.

Where they fail, and will keep failing

  • Anything needing taste that has not been written down. They will produce the average of the internet.
  • Anything needing a relationship. They cannot tell when a client is frightened.
  • Anything where being confidently wrong is expensive and no check exists.
  • Long chains with no verification. Four small 90 per cent steps is a 65 per cent outcome.

The trap to watch for

An agent that is wrong fluently is more dangerous than one that fails loudly. Your job is not to trust it more; it is to build the checks that make trust unnecessary.

The ability ladder

Where most work sits, from safest to hardest to automate:

  1. Retrieve: find the thing, quote it, cite it. Very safe.
  2. Transform: rewrite, summarise, translate, reformat. Safe with a review step.
  3. Draft: produce a first version of something you would have written. Safe with editing.
  4. Decide: choose between options. Needs explicit rules and an audit trail.
  5. Act: send, pay, delete, publish. Needs approval gates, always.

Most of the value in your first year is in levels 1 to 3. Most of the disasters are at level 5.

Your turn

  • List five tasks from your week, one per row, in position/agent-map.md
  • Put each on a rung of the ability ladder (1 to 5)
  • For each, name the check that would prove the work was done correctly
  • Mark one task you would hand to an agent this week, and one you would never hand over
  • Write one sentence on why the never-task stays yours

The check

The agent reads your file and confirms:

  1. Five real tasks from your week, not categories.
  2. Each has a rung from 1 to 5, and the rung is defensible against the ladder above.
  3. Each has a check that a machine could actually run, or an honest note that no check exists.
  4. At least one task is marked never, with a reason that is about the work and not about your feelings.
  5. The task you would hand over this week is level 1 to 3.

Failure signs: a page of abstractions, checks that read "I would review it", or a never-task that is only there because it feels risky.

Sealed when

position/agent-map.md passes the five checks and the map is written into your progress file with the rung counts, so later lessons can point back to it.
